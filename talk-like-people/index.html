Teaching a small model to talk like people

Chatbots get tested against fake users, and the fake users are too polite. humans& trained a 550B model to imitate people instead. I rebuilt the recipe on a 0.6B model and free GPUs to see which parts hold up.

Why imitate people?

Before a chatbot ships, it gets tested on conversations. Real users are slow and expensive to recruit, so another model usually plays the user. Usually that model is an assistant told to act like one.

That makes the test too easy. Assistants are trained to be clear, helpful and agreeable. People are terse, make typos, change the subject, and leave when they are annoyed. When researchers ran 451 real people through the same agent tasks as 31 simulated users, the simulated ones were too cooperative and too uniform, and agents scored better against them than against people.

humans& built Persimmon to play the people instead of the assistant, including group chats with several people at once. Their write-up describes the training in a few sentences and shares no code. I wanted to know how much of it works on a model about 900 times smaller, trained on Kaggle’s free GPUs.

What does “talk like people” mean?

A good assistant gives the best reply. A good simulated user gives a typical one, and over many conversations it gives every kind of reply about as often as people do. humans& call this matching the distribution.

Ask a group chat how its weekend was.

People answer “good”, “meh”, “worked all weekend ugh”, a photo, or nothing at all. A model that always gives the single most likely answer looks fine one message at a time and is wrong as a whole. A model that matches people gives each kind of answer about as often as they do.

So the test can’t be a score for one reply. It is a Turing test over many. A judge sees the start of a real conversation, then two versions of the next four messages: what people actually wrote, and what the model wrote. It picks the real one. The fooled rate is how often it picks the model instead. At 50% the judge can’t tell them apart. At 0% it always can.

I used two kinds of judge. One is a small classifier trained on half the test conversations to tell real from generated, then graded on the other half. The other is a large open language model shown a few labeled examples first, which is closer to what humans& did. Their fooled rates sit on different scales, so I only compare models under the same judge.

The recipe

Persimmon starts from a model that has not been taught to be an assistant, trains it on conversations between people, then trains it further against a judge. Here is the same shape at small scale.

A bug that hid everything

The first version wrote each message between the model’s chat markers, <|im_start|> and <|im_end|>. Every message the model wrote failed the same way: it never ended. None of 99 test samples was usable, so the judge-training step had nothing to learn from.

Those markers are in the base model’s vocabulary, but it was never trained on them. Each word in a model is stored as a list of numbers, and these two lists were about a third as long as normal ones, pointing almost exactly at the average of all the others. The model could read them but could almost never write them, and the cheap fine-tuning I used can’t change them.

The fix was a format the base model has seen many times: a plain chat log, one message per line.

What the model reads, shortened

## Setting
Ubuntu community IRC channel #xubuntu on 2015-04-28. Public text chat where people drop in to ask for and offer technical help.
## Participants
- nina
- tomas
- kai
## Chat log
<tomas> the screen goes black but the power button is still blue
<nina> tomas: maybe it's still plugged in?
<tomas> nina: i've tried without AC

A message now ends at a line break, which every model predicts well. With no training at all, the base model wrote 7 usable continuations out of 8.

After step 2 the model mostly stays with the people in the thread and sounds like them: short, lowercase, answering by name. It doesn’t follow the question yet. “Do you use disk encryption?” gets “yes”, then numbers.

Across 285 test conversations, step 2 moves almost every habit toward people’s.

Training against a judge

Step 2 only ever shows the model real conversations. When it writes four messages on its own, each one becomes the context for the next, so small mistakes pile up. humans& say their judge-training step is what made long conversations hold together.

Here is how that step works. The model writes eight versions of the next four messages. The judge scores each one. The model shifts toward the versions that scored better than their siblings. A penalty keeps it close to the step 2 model, so it can’t wander far just to please the judge.

Why a judge, and why one that keeps changing?

Nobody can write down what people would say next. A classifier can still learn to tell real from fake, and how confident it is says how far the model is from people. As the model improves, an old judge stops being able to tell. So it is retrained on the model’s newest messages, and the target moves with the model.

The first try made the model worse. The judge was weak, right only 55–67% of the time, and the model found a shortcut: shorter messages give a weak judge less to go on. Half of its messages became 31 characters or shorter. For people, and for the model before this step, that midpoint was about 42. The training judge stopped being able to tell real from fake. The new judge, which the model had never seen, caught it: the fooled rate fell from 40% to 30%.

That is why the final judge has to be a different one. A model graded by the judge it trained against gets credit for fooling that judge in particular.

The next run changed several things. The two that matter most for this story: train the judge until it is right at least 70% of the time on held-out conversations before training against it starts, and watch message length. If length drifts more than 25% from people’s, stop and go back to the last good checkpoint.

This time step 3 helped under both judges. Table 1 shows it didn’t get there by changing one habit. Naming the person you reply to rose to 38%, against people’s 37%, and made-up speakers fell to 3%. Messages were still a little short.

After step 3, the same laptop thread

tomas: no, just a usb drive that i forgot about
kai:   what's the drive brand and size?
tomas: it's 64-bit seahorse
kai:   oo

Now tomas answers kai’s question, kai follows up, and the thread keeps going. One detail, a “64-bit seahorse”, is something no person would write. This is one example, picked because it is short.

Where does it help?

If step 3 fixes the way mistakes pile up, its edge should grow with each message the model writes. In a later run, the model wrote eight messages, and the judge looked at the first one, the first two, the first four, and all eight.

That is the pattern humans& describe: training against a judge helped long conversations, not single replies. Another run showed the same thing on eight-message windows, under both judges: 33% against 12% with the classifier, and 25% against 17% with Phi-4, a different open language model.

I also tried a model about three times bigger, Qwen3-1.7B. It predicted held-out messages better, and step 3 again came out ahead on eight messages, 34% against 29% under Phi-4. That gap is within the noise. It also needed a bigger judge: the small one never got good enough to train against.

What I’d keep

  1. Start from a model that was never taught to be an assistant, and give it a format it already knows.

    The untrained base model wrote usable group chat once the format was a plain log. Chat markers it had never learned to write produced nothing usable at all.

  2. Training on real conversations gets the voice.

    Message length, naming people and staying with the people in the thread all moved close to people’s.

  3. A judge is only worth training against if it is good, and only if a different judge checks the result.

    A weak judge got fooled by shorter messages. A new judge caught it, and so did simply watching message length.

  4. With a good judge, it helps where mistakes pile up.

    The gain showed from the fourth message on, the same pattern humans& report.

The limits are large. This is a 0.6B model trained on one kind of conversation: technical help on Ubuntu IRC, mostly from the 2010s. The test sets are a few hundred conversations, so a gap of a few points is noise, and fooled rates for the same model moved between runs. Persimmon is 550B, trained on far more varied conversations and judged on eight-message group chats. None of these numbers can be set against its 19.8%.

Two open models are worth knowing about: UserLM-8B from Microsoft, trained on people’s messages to ChatGPT, and OSim-8B from CMU, trained across many kinds of human behavior. Neither is built for group chat. A companion note asks what a simulated student knows.
